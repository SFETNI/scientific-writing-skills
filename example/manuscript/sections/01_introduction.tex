\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Graphene-reinforced polymer composites have attracted considerable interest as
thermal management materials over the past two decades, driven by graphene's
exceptional in-plane thermal conductivity of approximately 5000~W~m$^{-1}$~K$^{-1}$
\citep{Balandin2011_ILLUSTRATIVE}.
In contrast, the polymer matrices used in these composites typically exhibit
conductivities in the range 0.1--0.3~W~m$^{-1}$~K$^{-1}$ \citep{Bigg1995_ILLUSTRATIVE},
creating a strong thermodynamic incentive to engineer the filler–matrix interface
for maximum heat transfer.

Despite two decades of experimental study, the relationship between processing variables
and composite thermal conductivity remains difficult to predict from first principles alone.
Effective medium theories \citep{MaxwellGarnett1904_ILLUSTRATIVE} provide analytical
bounds but do not capture the complex dependence on filler content, aspect ratio,
interface resistance, and matrix morphology simultaneously \citep{Chen2020_ILLUSTRATIVE}.
As a result, property optimisation has traditionally required extensive experimental campaigns,
which are both time-consuming and resource-intensive.

Surrogate modelling approaches—also known as metamodelling—offer an alternative path
for accelerating materials design \citep{Park2021_ILLUSTRATIVE}.
By training a data-driven model on a curated dataset of experimental or simulation results,
a surrogate can predict material properties at a fraction of the computational cost
of physics-based models \citep{Liu2022_ILLUSTRATIVE}.
Applications of surrogate modelling to composite thermal conductivity have been reported
for carbon-nanotube-reinforced matrices \citep{Kim2023_ILLUSTRATIVE}, but studies
specifically targeting graphene platelet composites remain limited.

In this work, we develop a gradient boosting regressor (GBR) surrogate model trained
on a dataset of 120 synthetic specimens characterised by eight input variables.
The objectives of this study are: (i) to evaluate the predictive accuracy of the GBR
model on held-out test data; (ii) to identify the most influential input variables using
feature importance analysis; and (iii) to characterise the prediction uncertainty
across the experimental design space.
